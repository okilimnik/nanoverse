Alcohols → the –OH group → hydration

Hydroxyl in Water

One ethanol molecule surrounded by six waters. The whole story is the –OH on the end: a hydroxyl group both gives a hydrogen bond and takes one, so bonds run in both directions at the same time. Drag the viewport to orbit.

Ethanol. Polar. –OH group both donates and accepts hydrogen bonds. Important in dissolving molecules and in holding structures together.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR

Count the directions. At rest four bonds are lit, and they are not all the same kind: one runs outward, from ethanol's own hydroxyl hydrogen to a water's oxygen, and three run inward, from water hydrogens to ethanol's oxygen. That two-way traffic is the hydroxyl group's whole character — the oxygen carries lone pairs to accept with, and the hydrogen is there to give away. Keep it in mind for the next slide, where a C=O can only ever do half of it.

Push the amplitude. The count breathes — 4 most of the time at the default, wandering between 2 and 5, and at maximum it dips to 0 now and then, the ethanol losing its whole hydration shell for a moment. A hydration shell is something continually made and unmade, not a fixed set of attachments.

LIMITS

The wobble is a hand-tuned sum of sine waves, seeded per atom — it looks like thermal jitter but it is not a physics simulation, and nothing here repels or collides. Two molecules can still drift through each other's space.

A bond lights up when the donor–H···acceptor distance is under 2.5 Å and the D–H···A angle exceeds 120° — the standard geometric proxy for a hydrogen bond, not a real electron-density calculation. Water's lone pairs aren't modeled as directional, so only the donor side of each candidate pair is checked.

The angle half of that test never actually fires on this slide. Each water is built with one O–H already aimed at ethanol's oxygen, and the wobble only slides the waters about without turning them, so every pair that comes into range is linear to within a fraction of a degree. What you are watching here is a distance cutoff. The next slide adds a tumble control, and there the angle test does real work.

Aldehydes → the –CHO group → hydration

Aldehyde in Water

Acetaldehyde — what the ethanol on the previous slide turns into, once alcohol dehydrogenase oxidises its –OH. Same two carbons, one group swapped. An aldehyde group is a carbonyl (C=O) at the end of a chain with a hydrogen on that same carbon, and neither half can donate a hydrogen bond: the oxygen has none to give, and the C–H is too weakly polar to count. Swapping –OH for –CHO turns a group that both gives and takes into one that can only ever take.

Acetaldehyde. Polar. C=O group is very reactive. Important in building molecules and in energy-releasing reactions.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR

Watch the GIVES readout. It is pinned at zero and stays there whatever you do with the sliders — not because zero is hardcoded, but because acetaldehyde has no polar hydrogen anywhere on it to offer. The previous slide's ethanol, the same size, sat at one. That single digit is the whole difference between an alcohol and an aldehyde in water.

Turn Tumble to 0. Both bonds lock on and stay on. Raise it and they start dying without the molecules moving apart — the hydrogen swings off-axis and the bond fails on angle alone. The dull red sticks are exactly those cases: close enough to touch, pointing the wrong way. A distance-only picture would count them as bonds.

Look at the methyl end. Two waters sit there and never bond to it, however close they drift. A –CH3 has no lone pair to accept with and no polar hydrogen to give. Only the C=O oxygen ever lights up — and it accepts at most two bonds, one per lone pair, against ethanol's four.

LIMITS

Both the drift and the tumble are hand-tuned sums of sine waves, seeded per water. They look like thermal motion but this is not a physics simulation — nothing repels, collides, or exchanges, and the molecule itself is held rigid. Real waters in a hydration shell swap places on a picosecond timescale; these six never leave.

A bond lights up when the donor–H···acceptor distance is under 2.5 Å and the D–H···A angle exceeds 120° — the standard geometric proxy, not an electron-density calculation. Lone pairs are treated as directions for placing the carbonyl waters, but the bond test itself does not check the angle at the acceptor, so a water can drift somewhere a real lone pair would not accept it.

Provenance is weaker here than on the ethanol slide. Acetaldehyde is too small and volatile to exist as a PDB ligand, so these are computed coordinates (PubChem CID 177) rather than experimental ones. They reproduce the published microwave structure closely — C=O 1.226 vs 1.216 Å, C–C 1.498 vs 1.501 Å, C–C=O 123.4° vs 124.1° — and the CHO group is planar to 0.0007 Å, which is what the lone-pair construction relies on. The C=O is drawn as a double line because the SDF says order 2, not because a list somewhere names that bond.

Ketones → the C=O group → hydration

Keto in Water

Acetone — the same carbonyl as the last slide, moved. An aldehyde puts its C=O at the end of a chain, with a hydrogen on the carbonyl carbon. A ketone puts it in the middle, with a carbon on both sides. Neither can donate a hydrogen bond. What the move changes is everything around the oxygen.

Acetone. Polar. C=O group is reactive, though less so than an aldehyde's. The smallest ketone body — what the liver makes out of fat when glucose runs short, and what you can smell on the breath in ketoacidosis.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR

GIVES is zero again, for a different reason. The aldehyde had a hydrogen on its carbonyl carbon and still could not donate, because a C–H is not polar enough. Acetone does not even have that — all six of its hydrogens are on methyl carbons. Two bonds come in, on the oxygen's two lone pairs, and nothing goes out. A carbonyl is an acceptor whichever end of the chain you put it on.

Now count the grease. Four waters sit against the two methyl groups and never bond to them, however far you push the amplitude. Tick shade the two methyl groups to see how much of this molecule that is. The aldehyde had one methyl and a bare hydrogen; the ketone has two methyls. Moving the C=O inward did not change what the oxygen does — it doubled the oily surface around it.

The difference that isn't visible here. An aldehyde's carbonyl carbon still carries a hydrogen, so it can be oxidised one step further, into the carboxyl group on the next slide. A ketone's carbonyl carbon is bonded to two carbons and has no hydrogen to give up: it is a dead end. Same group, different neighbours, completely different fate in a metabolic pathway.

Turn Tumble to 0 and both bonds lock on; raise it and they fail on angle while the molecules stay exactly as close as they were.

Drift and tumble are hand-tuned sums of sine waves, seeded per water. Not a physics simulation — nothing repels, collides or exchanges, and the molecule is held rigid.

Coordinates are the ideal (computed) conformer from PDB chemical component ACN, not an experimental measurement. Carboxylic acids → the –COOH group → ionisation

Carboxyl in Water

Acetic acid — a carbonyl and a hydroxyl on the same carbon. Put those two groups together and something neither one does alone starts happening: the O–H becomes acidic enough to let go entirely. This is the first slide with a pH knob, and crossing pKa 4.76 swaps one deposited structure for another.

Acetic acid. Polar, and ionisable. –COOH gives up a proton to become –COO⁻. Above its pKa it carries a negative charge — and in a cell, it always does.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR

Drag the pH through 4.76. The hydroxyl hydrogen disappears, a red charge cloud spreads over both oxygens, and GIVES drops from 1 to 0. That single readout is the whole difference: an acid that has already given its proton away has nothing left to give. Meanwhile TAKES goes up — a negative oxygen is a better acceptor than a neutral one.

Watch the C–O lengths. Below the pKa they read 1.208 / 1.342 Å: one double bond and one single. Above it they read 1.220 / 1.220 Å — identical. That is not a redraw, it is two different deposited structures (ACY and ACT) measured independently and reporting the same thing: once the proton leaves, the negative charge is shared across both oxygens and the two bonds become one kind of bond. Which is why the anion is drawn with one-and-a-half bonds rather than a double and a single — even though the file's bond-order column still says “double” and “single”, because a CIF has to pick a Kekulé structure.

Now set pH to 7 — roughly a cell. IONISED reads over 99%. When a textbook labels a group “carboxyl”, the thing actually present in the cell is the carboxylate. That is why aspartate and glutamate are drawn with a minus sign.

LIMITS

One molecule cannot show a mixture. The picture switches structure at the pKa, but a real solution at pH 4.76 is a 50/50 blend of both forms, and every molecule is swapping between them constantly. The IONISED percentage is the honest version of what the picture simplifies — it comes from the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, and it is continuous where the drawing is a step.

pKa 4.76 is acetic acid in dilute water at 25 °C. The same group buried in a protein can shift by several units.

The proton does not vanish. It goes to a water molecule and makes H3O⁺. That is not drawn, and neither is any counter-ion, so charge is not conserved on screen.

The charge clouds are formal-charge bookkeeping — half a minus on each oxygen because that is how resonance is taught — not a computed electron density. Bonds light on the same geometric 2.5 Å / 120° proxy as every other slide.

Amines → the –NH2 group → ionisation

Amino in Water

Methylamine — the same pH slider as the last slide, run in the opposite direction. A carboxyl is an acid: it loses a proton and goes negative. An amine is a base: it takes one and goes positive. And it pays for that proton with the one thing it had to accept hydrogen bonds with.

Methylamine. Polar, and ionisable. –NH2 picks up a proton to become –NH3⁺. Its pKa is 10.66 — well above cell pH, so in a cell an amine is almost always the charged form.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR

Watch TAKES, not GIVES. Drag the pH below 10.66 and the amine picks up a third hydrogen. GIVES climbs from 2 to 3 — and TAKES falls to zero. The nitrogen's lone pair is what was doing the accepting; a proton is now bonded to it. An amine trades away its ability to accept for one more hydrogen to give, and it cannot do both at once.

This is not special-cased anywhere. The slide hands the nitrogen to the same lone-pair routine every acceptor on every slide goes through, and a nitrogen with four neighbours simply has no free pair for it to find. The N LONE PAIRS readout is that routine's answer, not a label.

Set pH to 7.4 — blood. PROTONATED reads about 99.9%. Every lysine side chain and every protein N-terminus in your body is a cation, for exactly this reason. Pair that with the previous slide at the same pH, where the carboxyl was an anion: at cell pH, acids are negative and bases are positive simultaneously. The last slide in the deck puts both on one molecule.

LIMITS

The third N–H is constructed, not measured. PDB chemical component NME is the neutral amine; the ammonium form is made here by placing a hydrogen at the one tetrahedral vertex the other three bonds leave open, at a standard 1.010 Å. The rest of the molecule is not re-relaxed afterwards, though a real protonation pulls the N–C bond in slightly. Compare the carboxyl slide, where both forms are separately deposited structures and no construction was needed.

As on that slide, the drawing steps at the pKa while a real solution blends the two forms continuously — PROTONATED is the honest number. And the proton has to come from somewhere: it is taken from a water, leaving OH⁻, which is not drawn.

Same geometric bond test as everywhere in this deck (2.5 Å, 120°), and the same hand-tuned sine-wave motion. Not physics.

Phosphates → the –OPO3 group → charge

Phosphate in Water

3-phosphoglycerate — not a demonstration molecule but a real one, the product of the first ATP-making step of glycolysis. It carries three ionisable protons with pKas spread across the usable range, plus a fourth O–H that never ionises at all. Take the slider to 7.2 — cell pH — and read the net charge.

3-phosphoglycerate. Very polar, and multiply charged. A phosphate group is how cells tag a molecule: it adds charge, which adds water, which means the molecule can no longer slip out through a membrane. Important in energy transfer and in nearly every signalling switch.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR

Walk the slider from 0 to 14 and watch NET CHARGE. It steps 0 → −1 → −2 → −3, at pKa 1.4, 3.4 and 6.2. By cell pH this small molecule is carrying three negative charges. That is what a phosphate group is for: charge is a tag, and a molecule this charged cannot drift out through the greasy middle of a membrane. Phosphorylate something and you have locked it inside the cell — and given every enzyme a big, distinctive handle to grab.

The hydration shell grows with the charge. Bonds at the phosphate climb as the protons come off. This is the visible half of why phosphorylation changes a protein's behaviour so completely: you have not just added an atom, you have added a charged, heavily hydrated lump where there was a plain hydroxyl.

GIVES never quite reaches zero. Even at pH 14 one hydrogen is still there — the hydroxyl on the middle carbon. It is a plain secondary alcohol with a pKa around 15, off the top of the slider. Three of this molecule's four O–H groups look identical in the structure file and one of them behaves completely differently. Where a hydrogen sits does not tell you whether it will leave; what it is attached to does.

Park the slider exactly on 6.2 and MAJORITY SPECIES reads about 50%. The picture is showing you one molecule and having to pick.

LIMITS

The pKa values are approximate. They are literature ballparks for a phosphate monoester next to an α-hydroxy acid; real measurements shift by a few tenths with ionic strength, temperature and counter-ion, and sources disagree at that level. The shape of the ladder is the point, not the second decimal.

The coordinates are the fully protonated acid's, throughout. Removing a proton here removes an atom and nothing else — no bond length is re-relaxed. So in the file the P=O stays 1.480 Å while the two P–OH stay 1.609 Å, even at −3 where all three should have equalised near 1.5. The carboxylate has the same problem: its bond order is redrawn as one-and-a-half but its bond lengths are still the acid's. The carboxyl slide, which has both forms as separate deposited structures, shows what that relaxation actually looks like.

No counter-ions. In a real cell this ion is almost never alone — Mg2+ sits on phosphates constantly, and enzymes hold them with arginines. Take the size of that water shell as an upper bound.

Charge clouds are formal-charge bookkeeping spread over the oxygens that share it, not computed electron density. Same geometric bond test and hand-tuned motion as the rest of the deck.

Thiols → the –SH group → sulfur against oxygen

Sulfhydryl in Water

2-mercaptoethanol carries an –OH on one carbon and an –SH on the next: the same group, with oxygen swapped for the element directly below it in the periodic table. One molecule, and its own control. Then tick oxidise and watch sulfur do the thing oxygen cannot.

2-mercaptoethanol. Polar. –SH forms weak hydrogen bonds but strong, reversible S–S bridges. Important in holding folded proteins together — and this molecule in particular is the reagent used to take those bridges apart.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR

Compare the two bond lengths in the strip. C–O 1.430 Å against C–S 1.813 Å — the same kind of single bond, from the same deposited structure, nearly four tenths of an ångström longer. Sulfur sits one row down from oxygen and it is a much bigger atom. That is why it is drawn bigger here, and it is most of why a thiol behaves differently from an alcohol.

Both ends light up — and that is this deck's bond test failing. The geometric proxy asks only about distance and angle, and by those measures the S–H contact is a perfectly good hydrogen bond. It is not. Sulfur's electronegativity is 2.58 against oxygen's 3.44, so an S–H bond is barely polarised and its hydrogen is a poor thing to offer. Raise the minimum-strength slider and the sulfur bonds die first, in the dim teal, while the –OH bonds stay lit. That slider is both the lesson and the confession: the strengths behind it are looked up in a table, not computed from anything on screen, precisely because the geometry on screen cannot tell the two apart.

Now tick oxidise. Two thiols become one molecule joined by a real S–S bridge of 2.050 Å, and the two S–H hydrogens are simply gone — the count at the sulfur drops. This is the bond that staples an antibody together and holds keratin rigid, and it is reversible under ordinary cellular conditions, which no C–O or C–C bond is. Oxygen has no equivalent: peroxides exist but are not something a cell leaves lying around in a protein.

Watch the –OH ends while you toggle. They do not change at all. Half the molecule is having a redox reaction and the other half does not notice.

LIMITS

The reduced pair's pose is constructed. Each half is a real, whole mercaptoethanol (component BME, with its own real S–H) and the disulfide is a real structure (HED) — but the two free thiols are drawn in the pose their disulfide would have, pulled apart along the S–S axis, so you can see exactly which bond formed. In solution two free thiols have no fixed relationship to each other at all; they would diffuse apart.

The toggle is not a reaction. It swaps one structure for another. A real oxidation needs an oxidant, releases two protons and two electrons, and passes through a mixed intermediate — none of which is modelled. Nor is the reverse: mercaptoethanol breaks a protein's disulfide by exchanging with it in two steps, not by this switch.

The strength numbers are tabulated ballparks for typical neutral donor/acceptor element pairs (about 21 kJ/mol for O–H···O, about 7 for S–H···O). They are not computed from the geometry, they do not vary with distance or angle, and charged partners would be considerably stronger. Treat the TOTAL readout as a ranking device, not an energy.

Thiols also ionise — a cysteine thiol has a pKa around 8.3, low enough to matter at cell pH, and the thiolate anion is the reactive species in most enzymes that use one. That is not on this slide; the pH slider from the three previous slides would have been the way to show it.

The –CH3 group → the hydrophobic effect

Methyl in Water

Alanine — whose entire side chain is a single methyl group, the smallest hydrophobic side chain there is. This last slide is about a group that does nothing: no lone pair to accept with, no polar hydrogen to give, no pH at which it ionises, no charge ever. And that nothing is one of the strongest organising forces in biology.

Alanine. Non-polar side chain. –CH3 does not interact with water at all — which is exactly why water pushes it out of the way. Important in protein folding and in every membrane you have.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR

The readout that never moves. Bonds at the –CH3 stay at zero — at every pH, at every amplitude, for as long as you leave it running. Four waters are parked against the side chain permanently and not one of them ever does anything. Every other slide in this deck has had a number that responds to the knobs; this one is here because it does not.

Now move the pH slider and watch the rest of the molecule. The carboxyl end deprotonates at 2.34 and the amino end at 9.69 — the two previous slides, replayed on one molecule, in the right order. The methyl in between does not notice either one.

Stop at pH 7.4 and read ENDS. Net charge is zero, but the molecule is +1 at one end and −1 at the other: a zwitterion. This is what every free amino acid actually is in water, and it is why they are solids that melt high rather than oily liquids. Net-zero is not the same as uncharged, and the ENDS readout exists to keep those apart.

Why the nothing matters. Water cannot hydrogen bond to a methyl, so the waters next to one have to satisfy their bonds among themselves — in a more ordered arrangement than they would adopt in bulk. Order costs entropy, and the cheapest way for the system to pay less of it is to push the greasy surfaces together, out of contact with water. That shove is the hydrophobic effect: it folds a protein's core, it holds every membrane in your body together, and it is why oil will not mix with vinegar. Swapping glycine's no-side-chain for alanine's single methyl is already enough to change where a residue prefers to sit in a folded protein.

LIMITS

The most important limit in the deck is on this slide. The ordering just described is not modelled here. The waters at the methyl are placed at van der Waals contact and jiggled exactly like every other water on screen; real hydration-shell waters against a hydrophobic surface adopt tangential orientations so they can keep hydrogen bonding to each other. The entropy cost that is the hydrophobic effect does not appear in this picture at all. What you can see here is only the precondition for it — the absence of a bond. The force itself is a statistical, entropic thing that no single-molecule snapshot can show.

The third N–H is constructed, as on the amino slide: PDB component ALA is the neutral form, and the ammonium hydrogen is placed at the remaining tetrahedral vertex at 1.010 Å with nothing re-relaxed. The carboxylate's bond order is redrawn as one-and-a-half but its lengths are still the acid's.

The drawing steps between species at each pKa while a real solution blends them; pKa 2.34 and 9.69 are free alanine in dilute water, and both shift once the residue is inside a peptide. Same geometric 2.5 Å / 120° bond test and the same hand-tuned sine-wave motion as everywhere else — not physics.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR

Walk the pH from 0 to 14 on each residue in turn. Aspartate goes negative at 3.65, glutamate at 4.25, histidine at 6.00, lysine at 10.53, arginine at 12.48. Five residues, one shared chemistry — a proton moving — and nearly nine pH units between the first and the last. “Charged” is not a property a side chain simply has; it is a property it has at a given pH.

Now set the pH to 7.4 and click through all five. Four of them read over 99% charged and do not budge over any part of the slider a cell ever visits. Histidine reads about 4% — and, unlike the others, it is moving: nudge the slider to 7.0 and it climbs to 9%, to 6.0 and it is half and half. That responsiveness is why histidine turns up in the active site of enzyme after enzyme. At cell pH it is the only side chain that can pick a proton up and put it down again, because at cell pH it is the only one sitting on the fence.

Compare aspartate with glutamate. One CH2 apart, and the pKa moves by 0.6 — holding the same carboxyl one carbon further from the positively charged backbone makes it a slightly weaker acid. Then compare lysine with arginine: both positive, but arginine's guanidinium spreads its charge over three nitrogens and offers five N–H bonds to lysine's three. Watch GIVES.

Take lysine below and above 10.53 and watch TAKES. It jumps from 0 to 1. The nitrogen's lone pair was busy holding a proton; let the proton go and the pair is free to accept. That is exactly the trade the methylamine slide showed in the previous chapter — same group, same swap, now on a residue.

LIMITS

Only the side chain is hydrated. No water is placed on the backbone at all, even though the –NH3+ and –COO− ends are in reality the most hydrated part of a free amino acid. That is deliberate: it makes every number on screen a property of the side chain, comparable from one residue to the next. It also means the picture is not what a free amino acid in water looks like.

The backbone is held fixed at the cell-pH zwitterion (–NH3+ / –COO−) at every pH the slider reaches, so SIDE-CHAIN CHARGE is the side chain's alone. In reality the backbone titrates too, near pKa 2.2 and 9.5 — the methyl slide in the previous chapter is where that happens.

Arginine is drawn only as the cation. Its neutral form needs a choice about which nitrogen keeps the proton, and that choice would be an invention about a species you will never meet in biology. So above pKa 12.48 the CHARGED readout falls while the drawing does not change, and DRAWN says “one form only”. Every other residue here has both forms built from the deposited structure by removing a hydrogen, which is exact.

Two constructions, both stated. The third backbone N–H is placed at the remaining tetrahedral vertex at 1.010 Å, and each side chain is turned about its own freely rotating single bonds to the staggered position that leaves it least folded onto its backbone. Bond lengths and bond angles are untouched deposited values throughout; it is torsions that were chosen, because the deposited “ideal” conformer is one arbitrary pose and several of them curl the side chain onto the backbone hard enough to leave nowhere for a water to go.

pKa values are for the free amino acid in dilute water at 25 °C. Inside a protein the same side chain routinely shifts by two units or more — that shift is most of what an active site is. Charge clouds are formal-charge bookkeeping, not computed electron density, and bonds light on the deck's usual geometric 2.5 Å / 120° proxy. The drawing steps at each pKa; CHARGED AT THIS pH is the continuous, honest version.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR

The readout that says nothing. SIDE-CHAIN pKa reads none in water for all four. That is not a missing value — it is the definition of this class. A serine hydroxyl does ionise, at a pKa somewhere around 13–15, which is off the end of any slider in this deck and off the end of anything a cell does. Compare the previous slide, where every residue had a number.

Serine, then threonine. One methyl group is the only difference, and the whole hydrophobic story arrives with it: waters appear beside the –OH's waters that never bond to anything, however long you watch. Tick shade the greasy part to see which end is which. A single side chain can be polar and oily at the same time, in different directions.

Asparagine is the interesting one. Its oxygen TAKES and its two N–H bonds GIVE — both at once, from one small group. But the nitrogen never accepts anything, no matter where the waters drift. An amide nitrogen's lone pair is delocalised into the neighbouring C=O and is simply not available. Nothing on this slide special-cases that: the nitrogen is not declared an acceptor because it is not one.

Then glutamine. Same amide, one CH2 further out — exactly the step aspartate takes to glutamate on the previous slide. Biology keeps both members of each pair because reach matters: an interaction a side chain cannot stretch to is one it cannot make.

LIMITS

Only the side chain is hydrated, as on every slide in this chapter, so the counts compare across residues and the picture is not a real hydration shell. The backbone is drawn as the cell-pH zwitterion and held there.

The shells here are small, and smaller than the chemistry deserves. Serine's hydroxyl has two lone pairs and gets one water on this slide, not two — the second position lands inside the backbone carboxylate and is dropped rather than drawn somewhere false. The ethanol slide in the first chapter, with nothing else in the way, gets the full four bonds an –OH can manage. Take the counts here as what this crowded little molecule can fit, not as the group's capacity.

Coordinates are PDB Chemical Component Dictionary ideal conformers, with the side-chain torsions turned to the staggered positions that leave the residue least folded onto itself; bond lengths and angles are the deposited ones. The backbone ammonium hydrogen is constructed. Same geometric 2.5 Å / 120° bond test and the same hand-tuned sine-wave motion as the rest of the deck — not physics, and nothing here repels or collides.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR

Start with glycine, and move every slider. Nothing happens, ever. Its side chain is a single hydrogen: no lone pair, no polar hydrogen, nothing to ionise. The count is not zero because a threshold is never met — glycine declares no hydrogen-bonding site at all, so there is nothing to count. What that buys is freedom: with no side chain in the way the backbone can twist into angles no other residue can reach, which is why collagen is one residue in three glycine and why tight turns are full of it.

Then proline, and read BACKBONE HYDROGENS. It says 2 on N, not 3, and every other residue in this chapter says 3. The side chain has looped round and taken one of the nitrogen's bonds for itself. Put this residue inside a peptide and the count goes to zero: proline is the only residue with no backbone N–H to donate, which is why it cannot sit in the middle of an α-helix, and why it turns up wherever a chain has to change direction.

Now cysteine, and look at the colour of its bonds. They come up in the dim teal reserved for weak ones. Sulfur's electronegativity is 2.58 against oxygen's 3.44, so an S–H is barely polarised and a thiol is a poor hydrogen-bond partner — serine's shape with a quarter of the strength.

Finally, put cysteine and selenocysteine side by side at pH 7.4. Identical shape, one atom swapped one row down, and the pKa moves from 8.18 to 5.24. At cell pH cysteine is still a neutral thiol with only a trace of the reactive anion around, while selenocysteine is already fully ionised. That is the entire reason the 21st amino acid exists and gets its own translation machinery: an enzyme that needs a permanently armed nucleophile cannot get one from cysteine.

LIMITS

Cysteine's most important trick is not on this slide. Two thiols oxidise to a covalent S–S bridge — the one covalent bond a cell makes and unmakes inside a folded protein. That needs two molecules, and this slide only ever draws one; the sulfhydryl slide in the previous chapter is where the bridge forms.

Proline's real constraint is also not on this slide. What the ring actually locks is the backbone φ angle, and φ is defined by the previous residue in the chain. A single free amino acid cannot show it. What you can see here is the cause: the bond that would have carried a hydrogen is carrying a carbon instead.

Selenium is the deck's least-supported element. Its drawn radius, its water separation (3.55 Å) and its tabulated bond strength are extrapolated one row down from sulfur rather than measured. The coordinates are real — PDB component SEC — but treat the shell around it as an estimate.

As everywhere in this chapter: only the side chain is hydrated, the backbone is held at its cell-pH zwitterion, the third backbone N–H is constructed, and side-chain torsions are turned to staggered positions (which proline's ring, being a ring, refuses — it is drawn exactly as deposited). Bond strengths are tabulated ballparks, not computed from anything on screen. Same geometric bond test, same hand-tuned motion.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR

Start at alanine and walk to isoleucine. The side chain grows from one carbon to four, the shaded surface grows with it, more waters park against it — and SIDE-CHAIN H-BONDS stays at 0 the whole way. Push the amplitude to maximum and it stays at zero. There is nothing there to bond to.

That zero is the strongest force in protein folding. Water cannot hydrogen bond to a hydrocarbon, so the waters beside one have to satisfy their bonds among themselves, in a more ordered arrangement than bulk water would take. Order costs entropy, and the cheapest way for the system to pay less of it is to shove the greasy surfaces together, out of the water. That shove builds every protein core and every membrane you have. Compare HYDROPATHY across the eight: isoleucine at +4.5 is the residue water pushes hardest.

Now click methionine and watch its sulfur for a few seconds. A bond blinks in and out there — in the dim teal the deck reserves for weak ones, lit maybe a third of the time. A thioether really is a hydrogen-bond acceptor, just a feeble and unreliable one. Methionine is filed as nonpolar and that is not quite true; it is the first crack in this slide's label, and the next two chips are the rest of it.

Then phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan — the three big flat ones. Phenylalanine's ring does nothing, as advertised. Add one oxygen to the far edge of that ring and you have tyrosine, which both gives and takes, ionises at pKa 10.07, and whose hydropathy has flipped from +2.8 to −1.3. Tryptophan gives a bond from its ring N–H and never takes one, because that nitrogen's lone pair is spent holding the aromatic system together.

So the last three chips on this slide disagree with the label above them. That is worth more than a tidy chart: the four-way classification is a useful summary of a continuum, and this is where you can see the seam. A residue's behaviour comes from the atoms it is made of, not from the box it was filed in.

LIMITS

The hydrophobic effect itself is not modelled here. The waters at a greasy surface are placed at van der Waals contact and jiggled exactly like every other water on screen. Real waters against a hydrophobic surface adopt tangential orientations so they can keep hydrogen bonding to each other, and it is the entropy cost of that ordering that does the pushing. None of it appears in this picture. What you can see is the precondition — the absence of a bond. The force is a statistical, entropic thing no single-molecule snapshot can show.

Hydropathy is a tabulated lookup (Kyte & Doolittle, 1982), not computed from anything on screen. It is one of several published scales and they disagree with each other in the details; the ordering is the part to trust.

Aromatic rings are drawn with one-and-a-half bonds rather than the alternating single/double the CIF files record, because a ring's electrons are not arranged in one Kekulé structure — the file has to pick one and the picture does not have to agree.

As everywhere in this chapter: only the side chain is hydrated, the backbone is held at its cell-pH zwitterion, the third backbone N–H is constructed, and side-chain torsions are turned to the staggered positions that leave the residue least folded onto itself — aromatic rings, being rings, are drawn exactly as deposited. Tyrosine's pKa of 10.07 is reported but not drawn: this slide has no pH slider, so tyrosine is always shown in its neutral form.
